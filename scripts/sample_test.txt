Grug woke up because something was chewing his foot. Not nibbling. CHEWING. He opened one eye and found a mammoth calf staring at him like Grug was the world's worst snack. Grug kicked, yelled, and rolled out of the cave, which was a terrible plan because the ground outside was mostly rocks and one very angry bush.

The bush was not alive. Grug knew that. Probably. But yesterday it had scratched his backside, and today it was standing right where he needed to run. He punched it anyway. The bush won. Grug came away with leaves in his hair and the expression of a man who had just lost an argument to salad.

Across the clearing, the other cavemen were trying to invent breakfast. One guy held a rock. Another guy held a smaller rock and looked extremely proud of himself. Nobody had discovered bowls, so everyone was eating berries out of their hands and pretending the juice on their chin was war paint. Grug wanted meat. Real meat. Mammoth meat. The kind that made a person burp like thunder.

He grabbed his spear. It was basically a long stick with a sharp rock tied to it using a strip of something that might have been leather or might have been yesterday's lunch. The knot looked dodgy as hell. Grug tugged it twice, decided it was fine, and marched toward the river where mammoths sometimes came to drink.

The mammoth calf followed him. Of course it did. It had decided Grug was its new mother, bodyguard, and mobile scratching post. Grug tried to scare it away by making a terrifying face. The calf copied him. The other cavemen laughed so hard one of them fell into a pile of damp leaves. Grug promised to remember every single laugh when winter came and he was the only one with a warm cave.

At the river, the water looked calm, shiny, and suspicious. Grug had learned the hard way that calm things could be dangerous. Last week a calm log turned out to be a crocodile. The week before, a calm puddle swallowed his favorite sandal. He had only one sandal, and he still talked about it like a lost family member.

A huge mammoth stepped from the trees. It was all hair, muscle, tusks, and bad attitude. Grug crouched behind a rock and raised his spear. His hands were shaking. Not because he was scared, obviously. The wind was doing it. There was no wind. Shut up.

The mammoth sniffed the riverbank, stomped once, and looked straight at him. Grug froze. The calf waddled over and bumped its enormous head against the adult's leg. The big mammoth made a sound like a boulder falling down a staircase. Grug understood absolutely none of it, but somehow the conversation felt personal.

He threw the spear. It flew beautifully for half a second, spinning through the air like a legendary weapon. Then the stone tip came loose, sailed past the mammoth, and landed in the river with a tiny plop. Grug stared at the empty stick in his hand. Somewhere behind him, one caveman whispered, "Nice shot, b*tch." Grug knew exactly who said it.

The mammoth charged. Grug ran. He ran past the bush, which grabbed his leg with its evil branches. He ran past the rock pile, tripped on the rock pile, and invented three new swear words while falling. The mammoth thundered behind him, shaking dust from the trees. His entire hunting strategy had become: legs fast, brain quiet.

Grug scrambled up a slope and slid into a narrow cave opening. The mammoth stopped outside, snorting so hard that dirt puffed from the ground. Grug pressed himself against the wall, holding the useless spear like it might suddenly become a sword. The calf squeezed its head through the opening, blinked at him, and licked his ear.

"Get your slobbery face off me," Grug whispered. The calf licked him again. The big mammoth rumbled outside. For one awkward moment, all three of them were simply standing there, breathing, like a family meeting nobody had scheduled.

Then the mammoth turned and walked toward the river. The calf followed, but paused to steal Grug's last berry. Grug watched his breakfast disappear into a furry mouth and decided the universe was a damn joke.

Back at camp, the others demanded to know how the hunt went. Grug lifted the broken stick and said the mammoth had been too scared to fight him. The cavemen nodded with suspicious respect. One asked why Grug smelled like river mud and bush. Another noticed the missing spear tip. Grug told them it was a secret weapon, too powerful for ordinary eyes. They believed him for almost four seconds.

That evening, they tried making fire again. One caveman rubbed two sticks together until his arms shook. Another shouted advice from a safe distance, which was his main skill. Smoke curled up, then vanished. Everyone groaned. Grug sat down, exhausted, and accidentally knocked two stones together. A tiny spark jumped between them.

Everybody went silent. Grug knocked the stones again. Another spark. Soon dry grass caught, a little flame flickered, and the entire camp exploded into shouting. They danced around the fire like idiots, waving their arms and making noises that would embarrass a pack of wolves. Grug pretended he had planned it. Obviously. He was a genius. A genius with a bruised backside and no spear tip.

The fire changed everything that night. The cave felt warmer, the shadows looked less like monsters, and the meat someone had managed to find tasted better near the flames. Grug held his hands out and watched the orange light move across his fingers. For once, the dark beyond the cave seemed a little less ready to eat him.

Then something growled outside.

Every caveman stopped chewing. The calf, which had apparently followed them home, snored beside the entrance. The growl came again, deeper this time. Grug grabbed his spear-stick, stood tall, and tried to look fearless. His knees were doing their own little dance.

He stepped toward the entrance. The others hid behind him, which was a beautiful example of teamwork if you were a coward. Grug peered into the night and saw two bright eyes near the bush. The bush rustled. A small animal darted out, stole a piece of meat, and vanished into the darkness.

Grug lowered the stick. Nobody laughed. They were all too busy checking whether their hearts were still inside their chests. He cleared his throat and announced that he had successfully defended the tribe from a terrifying beast. Then he sat down before his legs could resign.

Tomorrow, he would fix the spear, find more food, and definitely not punch the bush again. Probably. The mammoth calf curled beside the cave and rested its trunk on Grug's foot. Grug sighed, looked at the fire, and accepted that this was his life now: one useless spear, one clingy mammoth, and a bush that had a personal grudge.

Still, the cave was warm. The river kept moving. The fire kept glowing. And for one strange night, Grug felt like the smartest caveman alive. Then the calf farted so loudly that the fire flickered, and the entire tribe woke up laughing. Grug stared at the ceiling and muttered, "Tomorrow I'm moving to a different damn century."
